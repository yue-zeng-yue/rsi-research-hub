## Research Problem

Can evaluation distinguish replay fitting, same-task transfer, harder-task transfer, related-task transfer, and cross-capability transfer rather than conflating them in a final benchmark score?

## Methodology and Algorithm

Tasks are generated as G_g(d,ξ) from game, difficulty, and seed. Held-out tasks are generated first; duplicate descriptors are excluded from non-replay refinement data. Methods refine through a bounded feedback interface without direct access to benchmark internals or held-out tasks. The G0–G4 ladder separates replay, new instances, harder instances, same-capability new games, and different-capability games. HCI=(s_after−s_before)/(s_reference−s_before), computed from mean target scores, expresses the fraction of reference headroom closed. Saved artifacts receive semantic annotations for modifications, applicability, and functional target. See §3 and Appendix A.

## Key Contributions

The contribution is an evaluation protocol, task generator, and trace-analysis framework. It makes broad transfer claims testable and shows that improvements can reverse across levels. It evaluates frozen-model artifact adaptation; it does not measure model-weight self-improvement.

## Experimental Setup

Six games: Farm and Combat, Sweepmine and Hero, MC and Astray, organized by planning, reasoning, and exploration. The first four support five difficulty levels; MC and Astray use five fixed evaluation tasks each. GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 appear in model comparisons, with Codex and DeepSeek Harness variants. Candidate validation remains within the refinement stream; replay does not itself trigger refinement.

## Benchmarks and Baselines

ACE, AHE, and HyperAgents represent experience-, harness-, and meta-level adaptation. General-purpose Codex, Claude Code, and DeepSeek Harness provide context. Scheduling ablations compare random medium, fixed-instance, mixed-difficulty, and increasing-difficulty streams. Implementations are benchmark adaptations, not direct reproductions of each original paper’s entire experimental setting.

## Limitations

Capability categories simplify interacting skills, so game transfer cannot establish professional-domain transfer. HCI is sensitive to small denominators and its chosen reference; values above 100% are possible and do not imply superhuman ability. Method-specific starting harnesses differ, complicating final-score rankings. Main tables provide point estimates without enough uncertainty to settle close comparisons. Semantic labels generated by an LLM are observational evidence, not causal attribution. Comprehensive dollar-matched method comparisons were not verified.

## Reproducibility

The MIT top-level repository documents Python 3.13, bundled methods, Docker for HyperAgents, game dependencies, benchmark/analysis/HCI commands, and output aggregation. Bundled HyperAgents retains CC BY-NC-SA terms; top-level MIT does not override them. Code and documentation were inspected; no installation or benchmark run was performed. Pins, seeds, exact task streams, reference scores, and annotation agreement are required for replication.

## Open Research Questions

How stable are method rankings across fresh game families and equal refinement expenditure? Can HCI uncertainty propagate reference-score uncertainty? Which edit categories predict durable transfer after controlling for task difficulty, starting competence, and evaluator artifacts?
